Two halves. First: build a classmate's object from their words alone. Second: distance drives form.

## The exchange

1. **Swap** — hand your printed procedure to the person opposite. Keep your diagram; they do not get to see it.
2. **Build** — 25 minutes. Follow the procedure exactly as written. Do not interpret generously.
3. **Stop where it breaks** — if a step is ambiguous, write down what was unclear, then make the most literal reading and continue.
4. **Compare** — put your result next to the author's diagram. The difference is the finding.

**Do not talk to the author. If you can ask them, the procedure is not being tested.**

### What to record

| As the builder | As the author |
|---|---|
| Every step where you had to guess | Which steps were misread, and how |
| What you assumed, and why | What you meant, in one sentence |
| A photo of what you produced | The rewritten step that fixes it |

**The usual suspects:** Which axis is the cylinder's height along? Does `move()` apply to the centre or a corner? Which way is positive rotation? Is the subtraction the right way round?

Formal notation removed most ambiguity. What is left is precise and fixable — that is the point.

## An attractor is a number

```
for each point p:
    d = distance(p, attractor)
    size = f(d)
```

That is it. Everything else is a choice of `f`.

**INDEX told you where in the sequence. DISTANCE tells you how far from something.** Both are just numbers driving a property — that is the only idea in this module.

### Three kinds

| | |
|---|---|
| **Single point** | `distance(p, a)` |
| **Multiple points** | min over all attractors |
| **Curve** | distance to nearest point on the curve |

## Falloff is the function

`f` is your decision. It is where the graphic quality actually comes from.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Linear** | `v = d` | Even change all the way across |
| **Inverse** | `v = 1 / d` | Sharp near the attractor, flat far off |
| **Smooth** | eased | Soft at both ends. Usually the best-looking |
| **Threshold** | on / off at a cut | No gradient at all. Two populations |

Two students with the same attractor and different falloff produce completely different images. **This is a design decision, not a setting.**

## Remap

Distance comes out in model units. Your property needs a specific range.

```
t   = (d − dmin) / (dmax − dmin)
out = outmin + t × (outmax − outmin)

d    0 … 8.4 units
out  0.1 … 1.0 radius
```

**Subtract. Divide. Multiply. Add.** Nothing here is new — it is the arithmetic from Week 2, applied to a distance instead of a coordinate.

Get the ranges wrong and everything is the same size, or one element swallows the sheet.

*1.1 requires your remap ranges documented on the sheet.*

## Two attractors, three answers

With more than one attractor, each point has a **list** of distances. How you reduce it is a design choice.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Min** | nearest one wins | Cell-like territories with a crease between them |
| **Max** | farthest one wins | A single central blob |
| **Multiply** | both, multiplied | Two peaks with a pinch between them |

Same two attractors in all three. Only the reduction changed. Most students never discover this exists.

*Adding the distances and averaging them produce the same field, just scaled differently.*

## Underneath, it is lists

Points, distances, sizes — three lists, same length, same order. Item `[2]` in every list describes the same element. Break that correspondence, by sorting or culling one of them, and the sizes attach to the wrong points.

**When a field looks scrambled, it is almost always a list that got reordered somewhere upstream.**

### When two lists meet

4 points, 2 attractors. How do they pair up?

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Longest list** | 4 results | The short list repeats its last item to keep up |
| **Shortest list** | 2 results | Stops when the short list runs out. Two points get nothing |
| **Cross reference** | 8 results | Every point against every attractor. Usually what you want |

**This is why your two-attractor field went wrong.** Longest List is the default, and it silently ignored your second attractor for most points.

Cross Reference gives 8 results for 4 points — which then have to be reduced back to 4 by min, max or multiply.

*Cross Reference may be used once per definition in this course. Beyond that, use graft.*

### Graft, flatten, wrap

Three operations that change list **structure** rather than list contents.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **Graft** | one list → one branch each | Each item becomes its own branch, so the next operation treats them separately instead of pairing them off |
| **Flatten** | many branches → one list | The opposite. Useful at the end, destructive in the middle — it throws away the structure you built |
| **Wrap** | `i mod n` | Indices become cyclic. Item 5 of a 4-item list is item 1 |

If flattening fixed it, you had a matching problem, not a flatten problem.

**Wrap is why a contour closes.** Connect point `[i]` to point `[i+1]` and the last one has no partner — unless the index wraps. That is Module 2 in one line.
